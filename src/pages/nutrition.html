Race-day nutrition: what to eat the week before

Carb loading isn't just a pasta dinner the night before. A practical timeline for the 7 days leading into race day.

You've put in the miles. The taper is underway. And now there's one variable left that can make or break your race: what you eat. The week before a race isn't about radical change — it's about strategic timing. Get it right and you'll line up with full glycogen stores, steady energy, and a stomach that cooperates. Get it wrong and you'll spend race morning in the porta-potty line wondering where it all went wrong.

This guide breaks down exactly what to eat — and when — in the 7 days before your race. No fads, no magic supplements, just practical advice built on sports nutrition science and tested by thousands of runners.

The carb loading myth

The classic "pasta party the night before" is one of running's most enduring myths. Carb loading isn't a single meal — it's a multi-day protocol that gradually increases your glycogen stores. Research from the Australian Institute of Sport shows that a 3-day carb load (starting 72 hours before race day) can increase muscle glycogen by 40-50% compared to your normal diet. One dinner won't get you there.

The key is reducing fiber and fat in the final 48 hours while increasing simple, easily digestible carbohydrates. Think white rice, pasta, bread, potatoes, and low-fiber cereals — not whole grains and cruciferous vegetables. Your gut will thank you at mile 8.

Day by day: the 7-day plan

7 days out

Eat normally. Focus on balanced meals with lean protein, complex carbs, and vegetables. Start increasing your water intake — aim for 2-3 liters a day. No dietary experiments this week. If you haven't eaten it during training, don't start now.

6-5 days out

Begin shifting your plate: 60% carbs, 25% protein, 15% fat. Still eating vegetables and fiber, but start favoring white rice over brown, peeled potatoes with skins removed, and white bread over whole wheat. Continue hydrating consistently throughout the day — not just at meals.

4-3 days out

This is your carb loading window. Push carbs to 70-80% of calories. Reduce fiber and fat significantly — think plain bagels, white pasta with light tomato sauce, bananas, white rice, sports drink. Aim for 8-10 grams of carbs per kilogram of body weight. For a 150 lb runner, that's roughly 550-680 grams of carbs per day. Add salt to your meals — you'll need the sodium.

2 days out

Peak carb day. Smaller, more frequent meals — 5-6 eating occasions. White pasta, white rice, bread, sports drink, bananas, plain crackers. Avoid anything spicy, fried, or high-fiber. This is not the day to try that new Thai place. If you're traveling to the race, pack your own snacks — airport food is a gamble you don't need.

1 day out

Lunch is your last big meal. Make it familiar — whatever carb-rich meal has worked for you before long runs. Dinner should be light, early (by 6pm if possible), and simple: plain pasta, white rice with a little chicken, or a baked potato. Stop eating 12 hours before race start. Sip water but don't force it — you're not trying to overhydrate, just maintain. One electrolyte drink in the afternoon if it's hot.

Race morning

Eat 2-3 hours before the gun. A small, carb-focused breakfast that you've tested in training: a bagel with a thin spread of peanut butter, a banana, and a sports drink. Or a plain oatmeal packet and a piece of white toast. Keep it under 400 calories. Sip water but stop 30-45 minutes before the start to avoid mid-race bathroom stops.

Caffeine? If it's part of your routine, keep it. If you've never used it before long runs, race day isn't the time to start. A small coffee 90 minutes pre-race is safe for most runners — but test it first.

What to avoid

×

High-fiber foods in the final 48 hours: bran, beans, cruciferous vegetables, whole grains with seeds. They bulk up your digestive tract when you need it empty.

×

Fried or fatty foods: slow gastric emptying and can leave you feeling heavy and sluggish. Skip the burger and fries victory lap until after the race.

×

Alcohol in the final 72 hours: dehydrates, disrupts sleep quality, and impairs glycogen storage. A single beer 4+ days out is fine. Closer than that, skip it.

×

New foods or supplements: no beetroot shots, no new gels, no "race day" powders you haven't used in training. The rule is simple — nothing new on race week.

×

Overhydrating: drinking too much plain water can flush out electrolytes and lead to hyponatremia. Drink to thirst, not to a forced schedule. Your urine should be pale yellow, not clear.

The best race-day nutrition plan is the one you've practiced. Use your long runs during training as dress rehearsals — not just for pacing, but for what you eat the night before and the morning of. Write down what works. By race week, you should be executing a plan you already trust.
