How to read your running form: a beginner's guide

You don't need a lab, a coach, or slow-motion video to spot form issues. Learn the four things to watch for on your next run — and what to do about them.

Running form is one of those topics that generates endless debate — and for good reason. There's no single "correct" way to run. Elite marathoners range from the elegant, forward-leaning glide of Eliud Kipchoge to the compact, high-cadence shuffle of Des Linden. Both are world-class. Both are "correct" — for them.

But while there's no universal ideal, there are universal fundamentals. Posture, arm swing, foot strike, and cadence are the four pillars that determine whether you're running efficiently or fighting your own body with every stride. The good news: you can assess all four on your next run without any special equipment. Here's what to look for.

Posture: head to toe alignment

Good running posture starts from the top and cascades down. Your head should be neutral — eyes on the horizon about 30-40 feet ahead, not staring at your feet or craning at the sky. Your shoulders should be relaxed and low, not hunched toward your ears (a common stress pattern that tightens your upper back and wastes energy). Your torso should have a slight forward lean from the ankles — not from the waist, which puts strain on your lower back.

Quick self-check: every few minutes, do a head-to-toe scan. Unclench your jaw. Drop your shoulders. Check that your hips are level — not sagging on one side. If you're audibly scuffing the ground, you're likely leaning too far forward. If you feel like you're braking with every step, you're probably leaning too far back. The sweet spot is a controlled forward fall that your legs catch naturally.

Arm swing: 90 degrees, forward and back

Your arms set the rhythm your legs follow. Keep your elbows bent at roughly 90 degrees and swing them forward and back — not across your body. Crossing the midline of your chest introduces rotational torque that your core has to counter, wasting energy and pulling your stride out of alignment. The motion should come from your shoulders, not your elbows, and your hands should stay relaxed — imagine holding a potato chip between your thumb and forefinger without crushing it.

A common cue: "hands pocket to cheek." Your hand should move from roughly hip level on the backswing to about cheek level on the forward swing. If your arms are swinging wider than that, you're over-striding with your upper body. Tighten the arc and let your legs match the new, shorter rhythm.

Foot strike: forefoot, midfoot, or heel?

The foot strike debate has calmed considerably in recent years, and the consensus is more nuanced than the old "never heel strike" doctrine. The truth: where your foot lands matters less than where it lands relative to your center of mass. A heel strike with your foot landing under your hips is biomechanically very different from a heel strike with your foot reaching out ahead of you — the latter is over-striding, and that's what causes braking forces and injury risk.

Most runners land with a midfoot or heel-first pattern, and that's fine. A small minority naturally forefoot strike, and that's also fine. What matters is that your foot lands under your body — not ahead of it — and that you're not experiencing pain as a result of your strike pattern. If you want to experiment with a different foot strike, do it gradually: 30-second intervals during easy runs, no more than 5% of your weekly mileage. Changing your foot strike too aggressively is one of the fastest ways to get injured.

Cadence: aim for 170-180 steps per minute

Cadence — the number of steps you take per minute — is the simplest metric to check and one of the most impactful to improve. Most recreational runners have a cadence in the 150-165 range, which typically indicates over-striding: taking too few, too-long steps. The target range of 170-180 steps per minute naturally shortens your stride, reduces ground contact time, and lowers the impact forces traveling up through your legs with every foot strike.

Count your steps for 30 seconds on your next run and multiply by two. If you're below 170, don't try to jump to 180 overnight. Increase by 5% at a time — from 160 to 168, for example — and hold that new cadence for a week before increasing again. Use a metronome app or a playlist in the 170-180 BPM range (we have one right here) to help your legs find the rhythm.

Self-assessment checklist: 4 things to watch on your next run

1.

Head and shoulders. Is your jaw tight? Are your shoulders creeping toward your ears? Every mile marker or traffic light, do a quick scan — unclench, drop, relax. It takes two seconds and it changes everything downstream.

2.

Arm crossover. Are your hands crossing the midline of your chest? If you can see your hands in your peripheral vision on both sides, they're probably crossing. Tighten the arc — keep each hand on its own side.

3.

Foot landing position. When your foot strikes, is it landing under your hips or out ahead of your body? If you're not sure, have someone film you from the side for 10 seconds on a treadmill. The difference is immediately visible.

4.

Cadence count. Count your right foot strikes for 30 seconds. Multiply by four. If the number is under 170, try shortening your stride slightly and see if the rhythm feels more natural. Don't force it — small adjustments over time are safer and stick better.

When to see a PT

Form adjustments are a tool for efficiency and injury prevention — not a substitute for medical care. If form changes cause pain that lasts more than two weeks, stop the adjustment and see a physical therapist who specializes in running. Persistent pain is your body's way of telling you something is wrong, and a good PT can do in one session what months of self-diagnosis won't accomplish.

Specific red flags: sharp (not dull) pain, pain that worsens as the run progresses, pain that alters your gait visibly, or pain that persists after 48 hours of rest. These aren't form issues — they're injuries that need professional evaluation.

Form work is a long game. The goal isn't to look like an Olympian on your next run — it's to make small, sustainable changes that keep you running healthy for years. Pick one of the four pillars above and focus on it for a month. Then add the next. By the time you're through all four, you'll be a more efficient, more durable runner — and you'll have built the awareness to spot issues before they become injuries.

This guide is for informational purposes and isn't a substitute for advice from a physical therapist, running coach, or medical professional. If you're experiencing pain or have a history of running injuries, consult a PT before making significant form changes.
